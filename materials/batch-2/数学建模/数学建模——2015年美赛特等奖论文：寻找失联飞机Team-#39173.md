## 一、摘要

### 摘要 1

**Abstract**

This paper proposes a model which determines the most probable locations of a missing transoceanic flight traveling from point A to point B. Since transoceanic flights are not detected by radar when they are more than 200 miles from a coast, radio communication and the occasional satellite imagery is essential to the construction of an efective search plan. Our search plan is built of the last distance in miles on the intended flight path (IFP) where Air Trafic Control heard from the pilot. We create a quadratic distance to casualty probability density function to predict the distance from the point of last contact to where the plane likely fell. Through trajectories and the possibility of veering of the IFP, a two-dimensional search region is determined and discretized into smaller areas. The trajectories account for any aircraft model, as parameters such as cruising altitude and Glide Ratio are assessed. Probabilities of containment (POC) are then assigned to each of these search areas, determining the probability the lost plane is within the given region.

We next consider the probabilities of detection (POD) during the search. We apply Koopman’s equation for the probability of detecting a missing boat. Furthermore, Koopman’s equation provides flexibility to input appropriate values pertaining to a given model of search plane and its instrumentation. Upon determining POD, we create an algo rithm to efectively search the bounded area. Our algorithm applies the POD to the POC’s and redistributes probabilities if the search plane is not found in a region while still considering the chance that the plane was actually in the region searched. If the plane is not found in a given cell, the algorithm outputs a neighboring region to search next. This customized neighborhood search plan maximizes resources while remaining time-eficient. An example of missing Transatlantic Flight ABCD is included as a demonstration of our methodology.

Our mathematically-derived search plan is built on a series of assumptions. These assumptions include that the IFP from point A to point B is linear and that the plane disassembled only upon impacting the water. Our model is sensitive to specific parameter changes but is therefore accommodating to diferent plane types. Strengths of the model include non-uniform probability distributions modeling distance to casualty and impact and a neighborhood-based, continuouslyupdating search mechanism.

参考解答：本段介绍了模型的核心目标，即确定失踪跨洋航班最可能的落点位置。由于跨洋飞行在距离海岸超过200英里时无法被雷达探测，因此无线电通信和卫星图像对于制定有效的搜索计划至关重要。搜索计划基于空中交通管制员最后一次从飞行员处听到信号的航程距离。作者构建了一个二次距离至失事概率密度函数，以预测从最后接触点到飞机可能坠落点的距离。通过轨迹分析和预期飞行路径（IFP）偏航的可能性，确定了一个二维搜索区域并将其离散化为更小的区域。这些轨迹考虑了任何机型，评估了如巡航高度和滑翔比等参数。然后为每个搜索区域分配包含概率（POC），以确定失踪飞机在该区域内的概率。

接下来考虑搜索过程中的检测概率（POD）。应用库普曼方程来计算发现失踪船只的概率。此外，库普曼方程提供了灵活性，可以输入与给定搜索飞机及其仪器相关的适当值。在确定POD后，我们创建了一种算法来有效地搜索有界区域。该算法将POD应用于POC，并在未在该区域找到飞机的情况下重新分配概率，同时仍考虑飞机实际上在被搜索区域中的可能性。如果未在给定单元格中找到飞机，该算法输出下一个要搜索的相邻区域。这种定制的邻域搜索计划在保持时间效率的同时最大化了资源利用。文中包含了一个失踪跨大西洋航班ABCD的例子作为方法论演示。

我们的数学推导出的搜索计划建立在一系列假设之上。这些假设包括：从A点到B点的IFP是线性的，且飞机仅在撞击水面时才解体。我们的模型对特定参数的变化敏感，因此适用于不同类型的飞机。模型的优点包括用于建模距离至失事和冲击的非均匀概率分布，以及基于邻域的持续更新的搜索机制。

## 三、引言

### 引言 1

**Introduction**

Since 1948, 83 aircrafts have vanished, some of which have still not been found (Bloomberg). Lost transoceanic flights are among the hardest flights to locate due to the lack of radar coverage. An average radius for radar coverage is approximately 200 miles in any direction (Hartmann). Because radar coverage is not an available method to track a transoceanic flight’s activity, Air Trafic Control (ATC) relies on radio or messaging communication with a pilot on a regular, predetermined basis. If there are no location updates in the designated time frame, signals alert Air Trafic Control that there has been a loss of communication, and that there is a potentially missing flight.

## 2 Model

Our model considers a lost flight, which starts at some location A and sets out to travel across the ocean linearly to its destination B. In order to develop an optimized search plan, we consider the probabilities of containment of the plane's whereabouts, the probabilities of detecting the plane while efficiently and conservatively using resources, and the continuously updating probabilities of the most likely location of the missing airplane.

### 2.1 Constructing Probabilities of Containment for a Missing Airplane and its Debris

The containment model maps the probability of the airplane's containment to specific regions surrounding the flight's last known position. Our first consideration in the containment model is estimating how far the plane most likely travels after a signal is lost. The variable $y$ is the last distance in miles from the starting location where air traffic controllers heard from the pilot. In addition to the mileage covered, it is important to take note of the initial latitude and longitude coordinates at $y_0$. It is assumed that the plane stayed on its Intended Flight Path (IFP) at this time. At this location, it is unclear if the plane continued traveling in the air or began falling to the ground. The Random Variable $Y_c$ refers to the distance to casualty, or the distance on the IFP at which the plane started to malfunction. It is assumed that when a plane malfunctions, its descent for the water begins. The distance to casualty is measured from $y_0 = 0$. We have modeled the probability density function of $Y_c$ as follows:

$$
f_{Y_c}(y_c) = \begin{cases}
\frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\left(y_c - (y_B - y_R)\right)^2 & : y_0 \le y_c < y_B - y_R \\
0 & : \text{elsewhere}
\end{cases}
$$

where $y_B$ indicates the remaining distance to destination B from $y_0$ and $y_R$ is the final mileage of the journey for which radar detection is available. Therefore, $y_B - y_R$ represents the flying distance left before Air-Traffic Control (ATC) radar detectors can spot the missing flight.

Our Random Variable $Y_c$ is constructed under the assumption that it is more likely for the plane to reach a casualty-state immediately or soon after the last communication with ATC. This intuition is reasonable because a loss of communication often occurs due to abnormal plane operation. The probability continues to decrease quadratically and becomes 0 in the radar-detective zone. The parameter $y_R$ can be determined using the equation below:

$$
y_R = \sqrt{(200)^2 - h^2} = \sqrt{40{,}000 - h^2}
$$

where $h$ is the cruising altitude in miles of the jet. Radar detectors can track planes within a 200 mile radius, on average (Hartmann), so $y_R$ can be determined by manipulating the Pythagorean Theorem.

We determine the possible locations where the airplane could have fallen based on a distribution of trajectories. The distance of impact, or the distance from $y_0$ at which the airplane hit the water is $Y_I$. This variable takes into account the glide of the airplane and the distance at which it is completely submerged and is modeled as follows:

$$
Y_I = Y_C + Gh
$$

where $G$ is the glide ratio of the plane (NASA). Each airplane model has an average glide ratio which is defined as

$$
G = \frac{\text{units of distance forward}}{\text{unit of distance downward}}.
$$

The glide ratio is multiplied by the cruising altitude to determine the horizontal distance the plane traveled during its downward fall (NASA). The probability density function of $Y_I$ can therefore be modeled using the following transformation of variables technique:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y_I < y_I) &= P(Y_C + Gh < y_I) \\
&= P(Y_C < y_I - Gh) \\
&= \frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\int_0^{y_I - Gh}\left(y_C - (y_B - y_R)\right)^2\,dy_C \\
&= \frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\left[\left(y_C - (y_B - y_R)\right)^2\,dy_C\right]\Big|_0^{y_I - Gh} \\
&= \frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\left(\left[(y_I - Gh - y_B + y_R)^2\right]\left[(0 - y_B + y_R)^2\right]\right) \\
\Longrightarrow\quad f_{Y_I}(y_I) &= \frac{d}{dy_I}\frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\left(\left[(y_I - Gh - y_B + y_R)^2\right]\left[(0 - y_B + y_R)^2\right]\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

After taking the derivative with respect to $Y_I$ we can conclude that the probability density function for $Y_I$ is:

$$
f_{Y_I}(y_I) = \begin{cases}
\frac{3}{(y_B - y_R)^3}\left(y_I - Gh - (y_B - y_R)\right)^2 & : Gh \le y_I < y_B - y_R \\
0 & : \text{elsewhere}
\end{cases}
$$

The probable distances from the point of lost contact to the point of impact are measured in line with the IFP. While this model has accounted for a single dimension, we can easily extend it to the left and to the right, indicating a plane's maneuver of course. The next portion of our model accounts for the possibility of veering off the intended linear path.

Veering away from the IFP is considered by assuming the segment of water representing the locations of impact are the average, or 0, and that the possible locations of the plane are normally distributed to the sides of $y_I$.

![Figure 1: One standard deviation is calculated to be the distance from the IFP where a plane gliding $\frac { \pi } { 8 }$ of course would land.](数学建模——2015年美赛特等奖论文：寻找失联飞机Team-%2339173.assets/图1.jpg)

Standard deviation of the normal probability distribution is determined by

$$
\sigma_ {x} = G h \sin \left(\frac {\pi}{8}\right)
$$

Since it is unlikely that a plane veers beyond $\frac { \pi } { 8 }$ from the $\mathrm { I F P } , \frac { \pi } { 8 }$ will be used to find one standard deviation. Thus the normal probability distribution perpendicular to the IFP is:

$$
f (x) = \frac {e ^ {\frac {- x ^ {2}}{2 (G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})) ^ {2}}}}{G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8}) \sqrt {2 \pi}}, \quad - \infty <   x <   \infty
$$

Because $f ( x )$ and $f ( y _ { I } )$ are independent distributions, we can create a joint probability density function by multiplying $f ( x )$ and $f ( y _ { I } )$ as follows: $f ( x , y _ { I } ) = f ( x ) f ( y _ { I } )$

$$
f (x, y _ {I}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {3 (y _ {I} - G h - (y _ {B} - y _ {R})) ^ {2}}{G h \sin \frac {\pi}{8} \sqrt {2 \pi} (y _ {B} - y _ {R}) ^ {3}} e ^ {\frac {- x ^ {2}}{2 (G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})) ^ {2}}} & : - \infty <   x <   \infty , G h \leq y _ {I} <   y _ {B} - y _ {R} \\ 0 & : \text {elsewhere} \end{array} \right.
$$

### 2.1.1 Partitioning Search Region Through Model Discretization

Given $f ( x , y _ { I } )$ , the search field can be divided into distinct regions. Each region is assigned a probability of containment $( \mathrm { P O C } )$ , indicating the probability that the region contains the missing plane and debris.

In order to discretize our model, we first consider X, the normal random variable. Since 99.7% of all observations are contained within 3 standard deviations of the mean, our search region is bounded on both sides by three standard deviations. We segment $y _ { I }$ over its entire range. Therefore, we consider $N - 1$ partitions and N vertical regions through the following equation:

$$
N = \left\lceil \frac {y _ {B} - y _ {R} - G h}{t _ {0 . 5} v _ {s}} \right\rceil
$$

where $t _ { 0 . 5 }$ represents the quantity 0.5 hours and $v _ { s }$ is the velocity of the search plane. This guarantees that each search plane spends up to half an hour sweeping a length of each search region. Assuming more than one plane searches a given region at a time, these partitions lead to a highly efficient search duration.

![Figure 2: The discretization method is modeled by the above $S A _ { N \times 6 }$ search region matrix. Note that in this case $N = 4$](数学建模——2015年美赛特等奖论文：寻找失联飞机Team-%2339173.assets/图2.jpg)

To find the probabilities of containment for each of the above rectangular regions, the joint probability density function $f ( x , y _ { I } )$ must be integrated over each region’s boundaries. In order to find $N \times 6$ probability values, a loop was created in Mathematica. The loop considers only the regions to the right of the IFP, since the normal distribution has the property of symmetry. The loop iterates through all N partitions in $Y _ { I }$ given each of the 3 set X regions. Below is the algorithm for the probability generation of each region within 1 standard deviation of the IFP:

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})} \int_ {0 (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} ^ {1 (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} f (x, y _ {I}) d x d y _ {I}
$$

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})} \int_ {1 (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} ^ {2 (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} f (x, y _ {I}) d x d y _ {I}
$$

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})} \int_ {(N - 1) (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} ^ {N (\frac {Y _ {B} - Y _ {R} - G h}{N}) + G h} f (x, y _ {I}) d x d y _ {I}
$$

The output of this loop is a list of probability values that can be matched to each of the search regions. Refer to Appendix B for the Mathematica Code. Now that we have determined probabilities of containment for each of these ocean search regions, we must obtain the boundaries of each region in latitude and longitude coordinates to facilitate the search and rescue team’s efforts.

### 2.1.2 Finding Latitude and Longitude Coordinates for Search Regions

Initially, the bounds of a given uniform probability region $r _ { u \times l }$ can be represented as $0 \leq u \leq N$ and $- 3 \leq l \leq 3$ where $l ,$ u are integer multiples of n = region number along the IFP, $\sigma _ { x }$ respectively.

![Figure 3: The location of a given $r _ { u \times l } = ( l , u )$ in reference to an IFP and $\sigma _ { x }$](数学建模——2015年美赛特等奖论文：寻找失联飞机Team-%2339173.assets/图3.jpg)

In order to reference a discretized probability region to the IFP and y , we must define a coordinate axis with a y-axis along the IFP, an x-axis in the direction of $\sigma _ { x }$ and an origin at $( y _ { I } , 0 \sigma _ { x } )$ . Now the bounds of a region $r _ { u \times l }$ in miles are:

### 2.1 Coordinate Transformation

$$
- l (G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8})) \leq x \leq l (G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8}))
$$

and

$$
0 \leq y \leq \frac {u (| | \mathbf {y _ {B}} - \mathbf {y _ {0}} | | - G h)}{N}.
$$

To translate any given number $m$ of zone-bounding coordinates in $(x, y)$ to latitude and longitude, build a $2 \times m$ matrix

$$
C = [ c _ {1} c _ {2} c _ {3} \dots c _ {m} ]
$$

where each bounding coordinate $\mathbf {c _ { i } }$ where $0 < i \le m$ is of the form

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} c _ {x} \\ c _ {y} \end{array}\right].
$$

Next, using a rotation matrix, vector addition and vector scaling will effectively shift an “IFP-referenced” coordinate to latitude and longitude. $\mathbf {y _ {0}}$ and $\mathbf {y}$ are known in the “IFP-referenced” system and in traditional coordinates. The components of $\mathbf {y _ {B}} - \mathbf {y _ {0}}$ in latitude and longitude are designated $r _ {e}, r _ {n}$. To build a rotation matrix from the “IFP-referenced” system to latitude and longitude, we first must find the counter-clockwise angle $\phi$ between the two coordinate systems as follows:

$$
\phi = \arctan \bigl (\frac {r _ {e}}{r _ {n}} \bigr).
$$

Using $\phi$, the rotation matrix $R$ is either:

$$
R = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \cos \phi & \sin \phi \\ - \sin \phi & \cos \phi \end{array}\right]: \frac {\pi}{2} \leq \phi <   0
$$

or

$$
R = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \cos \phi & - \sin \phi \\ \sin \phi & \cos \phi \end{array}\right]: 0 \leq \phi \leq \frac {\pi}{2}.
$$

One way to test whether the rotation matrix is calculated correctly is to verify that $\det(R) = 1$. After finding a suitable $R$, multiply $C$ by $R$ to express every $c$ in $C$ in miles of latitude and longitude with reference to $\mathbf {y _ {0}}$, where $\rho$ is the average distance in miles of one degree North and one degree East from $\mathbf {y _ {0}}$. Finally, add the resulting vector to $\mathbf {y_0}$ to have coordinates in degrees North and East. In summary, changing $C$ in the “IFP-referenced” system to the corresponding coordinate matrix $C _ {L}$ in latitude and longitude amounts to:

$$
C _ {L} = \rho R C + \mathbf {y _ {0}}.
$$

### 2.2 Probability of Detection

A standard model for finding the Probability of Detection (POD) is the exponential function

$$
P O D = 1 - e ^ {- \mathrm{coverage}}
$$

where coverage is the area effectively swept divided by the entire search area $A _ {S}$ (Frost 1999). To determine our region’s POD, we use a slight variation of B.O. Koopman’s Exponential Model. The search area is defined as

$$
A _ {S} = G h \sin (\frac {\pi}{8}) \left(\frac {y _ {B} - y _ {R} - G h}{N}\right),
$$

where the $A _ {S}$ represents the area of a single partitioned region of the overall search region. The area effectively swept is dependent on the effective sweep width $S$ and the effort. The effective sweep width depends on the effective detection distance $\kappa \epsilon$ of the instrument (eg. human eyes, radar, etc.) used for the search, and the elevation of the cruising search plane is $h _ {s}$. We modify Koopman’s model to account for variations in weather, conditions, search abilities and more. This variation is denoted by the scalar $\kappa$, which takes values between 0 and 1. The distance from the search plane’s path projected on the ocean to the furthest possible detected object is $\sqrt { ( \kappa \epsilon ) ^ {2} - h _ {s} ^ {2} }$. Assuming the search plane flies in parallel swaths, the effective sweep width would be

$$
S = 2 \sqrt {(\kappa \epsilon) ^ {2} - h _ {s} ^ {2}}
$$

in order to detect all of $A _ {S}$. The effort is said to be $d _ {s} n$ where the distance searched by an individual aircraft per swath is $d _ {s}$ and the number of search planes included in the search is $n$. Assuming the search plane flies parallel to the IFP, the number of swaths the plane must fly is the number of subregions $N$ so $d _ {s}$ is

$$
d _ {s} = \left(\frac {y _ {b} - y _ {r} - G h}{N}\right).
$$

In order to search simultaneously and most efficiently, the planes are assumed to be flying at the same velocity. Thus $d _ {s}$ is the same for each plane since they begin searching simultaneously. Incorporating each of these components into a single model yields the following POD equation:

$$
P O D = 1 - e ^ {- 2 \frac {d _ {s} n \sqrt {(\kappa \epsilon) ^ {2} - h _ {s} ^ {2}}}{G h \sin \frac {\pi}{8} \frac {(y _ {B} - y _ {R} - G h)}{N}}}
$$

Unless search effort is changed based on the search region, it is assumed that the POD is uniform among all regions of probable containment.

## 二、方法论与模型构建

### 小节 2.3 POC更新算法

若不存在仪器误差或人为误差，探测概率（POD）将等于 1，从而无需调整离散化区域的包含概率（POC）。由于搜索条件远非完美，我们将 $1 - POD_q$ 定义为失事飞机在区域 $q$ 中未被发现的概率。

既然我们在搜索区域 $q$ 后未找到飞机，我们需要更新该区域的 POC：

$$
POC_{q'} = (POC_q)(1 - POD_q).
$$

这是指飞机实际上位于已搜索区域内但未被发现的情况。假设所有离散化区域的总包含概率保持不变，当 $POC_q$ 变为 $POC_{q'}$ 时，损失的概率必须分摊到其他区域。为此，我们将以下数值加到除 $POC_{q'}$ 以外的每个 POC 值上：

$$
\frac{POC_q - PO C_{q'}}{6N - 1}
$$

此过程可以在搜索期间迭代执行，以更新每个已搜索离散概率区域的 POC 值。

### 小节 2.3.1 使用POC更新算法确定搜索模式

为了确定搜索 $6 \times N$ 个不同区域的最佳顺序，我们利用上述 POC 更新算法，并通过梯度选择下一个搜索区域。“最佳”顺序是指在资源和时间方面最有效率的顺序。我们希望用最少的飞行时间和燃料覆盖整个地图。因此，我们要最小化在非相邻区域之间的飞行，因为这会增加无用或冗余的覆盖范围，并增加覆盖整个区域所需的时间。

搜索计划算法包括在完成一个区域的搜索后更新 POC 地图，以找到新的包含概率地图（假设坠毁飞机未在最近搜索的区域被发现）。

在 Mathematica 中，我们使用矩阵表示 POC 地图。创建了一个 Mathematica 循环来评估每次迭代中刚搜索过的区域 $k$ 与其相邻邻居之间更新的 POC 差异。我们取这些差异的最小值，然后定位该最小元素的行 $n_{new}$ 和列 $\sigma_{new}$，以找到下一个要搜索的区域 $r_{n_{new}, \sigma_{new}}$。

本质上，这种方法包括：更新 POC 矩阵，计算梯度/差异以识别最高的相邻 POC，并将该区域作为下一个被搜索的目标。

### 小节 2.4 方法论中的假设

我们的模型及由此产生的搜索计划建立在以下几个假设之上：

- 失事飞机的预定航线是从 A 点到 B 点的直线路径。
- 失事飞机的滑翔效应影响了其进入海洋的轨迹。飞机在空中不会爆炸。
- 任何碎片在撞击水面时都会解体。
- 飞机偏离初始飞行路径（IFP）的角度不超过 $3G \sin(\theta)$。（注：原文此处符号可能有误，通常指偏航角度限制）
- 没有外力（如洋流）将碎片或飞机从它们首次着陆的地方移走。
- 模型中的所有飞机（失事飞机和搜索飞机）都以恒定速度飞行。
- 失事飞机更有可能在最后通信点附近被发现。
- 失事飞机在整个搜索行动期间保持漂浮状态（可检测）。
- 每架搜索飞机拥有相同的仪器，以相同的速度搜索，并以相同的高度飞行。

### 小节 2.5 敏感性分析

为了测试参数变化如何影响我们的模型，我们讨论了特定参数及其对模型组件和搜索计划的影响之间的关系。

我们通过考虑 $f(G)$ 和 $f(\theta)$ 来评估参数变化对 $f(x, y_I)$ 的影响。通过固定随机变量 $x$ 和 $y_I$ 进行实验发现，$f(G)$ 和 $f(\theta)$ 的曲线形状非常相似。

随着 $G$ 和 $\theta$ 的增加，相应的 $f(G)$ 和 $f(\theta)$ 迅速下降。由于 $f(x, y)$ 的有界积分产生 POC，$G$ 和 $\theta$ 对 POC 的影响与它们对 $f(x, y)$ 的影响相同。简而言之，随着 $G + \theta$ 的减小，发现失事飞机的概率增加。

**1. 参数 $\theta$ 的影响**

增加 $\theta$ 会直接增加搜索面积，因为 X 距离是以英里为单位的倍数 $\sigma_x = G h \sin(\theta)$ 来测量的，这与所选的 $0 \leq \theta \leq \frac{\pi}{2}$ 成正比。以下图表是使用工作示例（第 3 节）中发现的恒定参数值生成的。

**2. 滑翔比的影响**

增加滑翔比对 POD 的影响类似于增加 $\theta$。

**3. 指数分母的影响**

增加指数的分母将对 POD 产生反向影响。因此，遵循工作示例中解释的方法论的搜索队更有可能找到滑翔比较小的飞机。

## 二、模型的强度与弱点

### 2.6 模型的强度与弱点

在实际应用中，增加搜索飞机的数量将提高探测到的可能性。

由于探测概率（POD）方程中包含元素 $\sqrt { ( \kappa \epsilon ) ^ { 2 } - h _ { s } ^ { 2 } }$ ，如果 $h _ { s } ^ { 2 } > ( \kappa \epsilon ) ^ { 2 }$ ，则可能会出现负数的平方根。飞机必须飞得足够低，以便仪器有效范围 $(\kappa\epsilon)$ 能够到达地面。否则，飞机无法探测到其下方的任何物体，因此POD实际上为零。因此，当 $h _ { s } ^ { 2 } > ( \kappa \epsilon ) ^ { 2 }$ 时，在条件改善之前进行搜索是没有意义的。

显然，我们的模型对几个参数的变化非常敏感，因此可能不被视为一个稳健的概率模型。然而，对输入参数变化的敏感性是必要的，以便使模型适应不同类型飞机和搜索仪器的规格。

#### 2.6.1 强度

我们概述的模型具有其他搜救方法所不具备的特定优势。首先，我们使用二次概率密度函数，而不是常用的均匀分布。在许多搜救方法中，假设事故时间在一个给定范围内所有可能的时间内均匀分布（Lawrence 1977）。在这里，我们仔细构建了一个递减且凹向下的分布，以解释在 $y _ { 0 }$ 之后早期出现并发症的大概率。我们的模型还考虑了飞行轨迹，以映射事故发生时飞机的运动到海洋表面的区域。基变换允许搜索者使用实际的经纬度坐标来定位搜索区域，而不是跟踪与失去联系点的跟踪角度和距离。POD结合了多个因素，包括一个附加常数 $k$。

![Figure 9: The joint density function is shown above, indicating the locations in which the $\mathrm { P O C }$ is largest, and locations where the $\mathrm { P O C }$ is smallest](数学建模——2015年美赛特等奖论文：寻找失联飞机Team-%2339173.assets/图4.jpg)

当 $k = 1$ 时，搜索在完美条件下进行，而 $k = 0 . 5$ 可能表示有雾、波涛汹涌的水面或其他抑制因素。这个常数有助于更好地解释覆盖率，同时仍使用Koopman常用的指数模型。

我们的搜索计划是一个不断更新算法，同时兼顾时间效率。如果在高概率区域未检测到物品，则更新其他搜索区域的POC（Point of Contact Probability，此处指代该区域的发现概率或相关指标）。在被搜索区域的“邻域”中，概率次高的单元格将被调查。实施邻域限制加快了搜索过程，不浪费旅行时间，同时仍然搜索具有高POC的区域。我们的搜索计划可以轻松推广到不同速度的搜索飞机以及更大或更小的海洋区域。

#### 2.6.2 弱点

重要的是要考虑我们模型的弱点，这些弱点可能在将来值得解决。首先，最接近Gh但在X方向上超过1个标准差的区域的POC略微分配过多。标准差是使用三角学计算的，表明我们的矩形概率区域是基于三角形计算得出的。参见图1。由于这种过度分配，在IFP的1个标准差之外，朝向Y<sub>I</sub>的后部分区的概率计算略有不足。

另一个需要考虑的弱点是在每个划分的搜索区域中没有考虑洋流的影响。如果有更多时间，我们希望扩展表面洋流对定位碎片和其他飞机残骸的影响。

### 4 Conclusion

The final results of our model present a valid, time-efficient search plan for locating missing flight ABCD from Boston to Portugal. Our methodology accounts for all sizes and types of airplanes as well as all types of search planes. Our sensitivity analysis stresses the importance of careful consideration of resources and the number of search planes. A greater number of search planes drastically increases the probability of detecting the missing plane. While some parameters are more robust than others, carefully choosing the number of search planes per square will optimize time-efficiency and overall detection. Our neighborhood-based search plan makes this model a strong choice when considering especially large search regions. While we did not have time, we hoped to incorporate the impacts of currents in the movement of airplane debris. We plan to further explore this topic to eventually extend our model to account for debris search plans too.

### 5 Summary for an Airline’s Press Conference

There has been a new model proposed for locating missing transoceanic flights, such as Malaysian Flight 370. Since 1948, 83 aircrafts have vanished, some of which have still not been found (Bloomberg). In the case of Malaysian flight 370, not enough information was available on the plane’s modified path, to effectively use the Search and Rescue models currently in place(Seidel). This new mathematical model narrows the search region of missing planes and predicts their location of impact with the water, considering deviations from their intended paths. The model incorporates a time-efficient search method, allocating time and effort to the most likely locations of a missing transoceanic flight traveling from point A to point B.

Since transoceanic flights are not detected by radar when they are more than 200 miles from a coast, radio communication and the occasional satellite imagery is important in tracking the off-the-radar plane. These mathematicians created a search plan which is based on the last distance in miles on the intended flight path (IFP) where Air Traffic Control heard from the pilot. The mathematicians assign probabilities to different search regions. The model accounts for the likelihood that the plane fell closer to the distance of last contact rather than farther away, since loss of contact often indicates a mechanical issue or takeover of the plane. They use physics, primarily modeling the gliding of planes from the sky, to determine a search area. The trajectories of the planes falling from the sky can be calculated for any aircraft model, as they determine these distances using measurements of the cruising altitude and the Glide Ratio of a given plane. Basically, after performing several calculations, Probabilities of containment (POC) are assigned to each of these search areas. The probabilities will tell searchers how likely it is that the missing plane (or its parts) lie in that region.

After mapping the likelihoods that the plane is in each of the regions, the mathematicians account for the probabilities that the search equipment will actually detect the plane. The simple equation for the probability of detection makes it easy to quickly input values like the speed of a search plane, a search plane’s radius, the number of search planes and more. If this probability is not as high as the team would like, it can easily be increased by adding more search parties to the team.

Once the searchers know their probability of detecting the lost plane, the mathematicians use a new algorithm to search the bounded area. This algorithm applies the probability of detecting the plane to each rectangular region which has a certain chance of containing the plane. If a plane is not found in a given region, the algorithm determines an appropriate neighboring region to search, making the search plan very time efficient, and the process continues until the plane is located. There is still a chance that the plane was missed in a given region, so the search plan includes the possibility of revisiting certain areas later on. This customized neighborhood search plan maximizes resources while remaining time-efficient.

Of course we must use this search plan with a few restrictions in mind. If the path from destination A to destination B is not a straight line, this method becomes more difficult to use. The mathematicians note that while statistics are an incredibly powerful tool, there are always assumptions to consider, such as up to how far the plane could have veered from its intended path. This model is a breakthrough in Search and Rescue Theory and will be implemented when needed in the future.
